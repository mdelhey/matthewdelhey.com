Visions of Community at the University of Berlin: Kant, Fichte, Schleiermacher

Matthew J. Delhey, April 9, 2026 (University of Texas at Dallas)  

1.   Introduction

On September 5, 1807, the idealist philosopher Johann Gottlieb Fichte received an extraordinary letter from Karl Friedrich von Beyme, Prussia’s chief cabinet officer. Beyme had been commissioned by King Friedrich Wilhlem the Third to “establish a general educational establishment [Lehranstalt] in Berlin,” and wrote the following in an enthusasitic letter to Fitche (Köpke 1860, 163):

[P1] It will certainly be very welcome news to your Honor that His Majesty the King has decided to establish a general teaching establishment in Berlin. […] Such an establishment in Berlin has long been my favorite idea. Now necessity is bringing it to fruition. You will certainly believe, without my express assurance, that I am counting particularly on your advice and support in this regard. No one feels as keenly as you what we need, and no one perceives this in its universality as you do. I therefore sincerely request that you direct your thoughts toward the most expedient implementation of the Royal intention. Neither the use nor the abuse that old establishments have to contend with imposes any shackles on us. Your mind can develop completely free from all constraints, and therefore I expect a perfect whole. (GA 3,6:173)

Fichte would not disappoint Beyme. Reassuring the privy councilor that [P2] “an organic whole already stands before my soul, and it only needs the pen to grasp it, which I will seize without hesitation” (GA 3,6:177), Fichte submitted his lengthy proposal to the minister in five installments throughout October 1807, entitled Deduced Plan of a Higher Educational Establishment to Be Instituted in Berlin (Deducirter Plan einer zu Berlin zu errichtenden höhern Lehranstalt).

However, Fichte did not arrive at his proposal in isolation. Kant’s publication of the Conflict of the Faculties in 1797, combined with a general malaise surrounding German universities, had spurred a series of public reflections on the university and calls for reform by the leading intellectuals of the day. The issue was exacerbated by Prussia’s defeat by Napoleon in the Battle of Jena in 1806 and Napoleon’s subsequent closure of the University of Halle, leading the King to famously declare that [P3] “the state must replace with intellectual strength what it has lost in material resources.” It was this context in which Beyme had commissioned proposals for the new University of Berlin not only from Fichte but also two other faculty members from the University of Halle.

However, Friedrich Schleiermacher, an important idealist philosopher and previous member of Halle’s theology faculty, was not among the invitees. Catching wind of Beyme’s request from his Halle colleagues, and not without some indignation, Schleiermacher hastily composed his own treatise on the university, Occasional Thoughts on Universities in the German Sense (Gelegentliche Gedanken über Universitäten in deutschem Sinn), which he published in March 1808.

Finally, between 1808 and 1809, Wilhelm von Humboldt would synthesize these various proposals in his now-famous memorandum, On the Internal Structure of the University in Berlin (Über die innere und äussere Organisation der höheren wissenschaftlichen Anstalten in Berlin). When classes began at the University of Berlin in October 1810, it was Humboldt’s plan that won the day. While other proposals for the new university had circulated, Schleiermacher’s and, to a lesser degree, Fichte’s proved to be the most influential on Humboldt.

Beyme’s request, and Fichte’s and Schleiermacher’s plans, and Humboldt’s implementation, are remarkable, even shocking. It is difficult to imagine another instance in the history of philosophy in which leading thinkers were granted such a direct influence in redesigning an institution from the ground up and enjoyed so much success in having their designs realized. The model of higher education that emerged from this episode, known today as the “Humboldtian model of higher education” or simply the modern research university, has become paradigmatic in Germany and Europe, and has been immensely influential in North America and throughout the world. Moreover, as Beyme’s letter makes clear, this model was not merely designed by philosophers but, more importantly, by philosophy; each philosopher was tasked not only with redesigning the university from the ground up, but to do so in light of his philosophical commitments, or, as Fichte puts it, on the basis of his “general philosophical education” [allgemeiner philosophischer Bildung] (GA 3,6:181). What is fascinating about this episode in classical German philosophy is precisely this imbrication of idealism and institutional design.

Unsurprisingly, Fichte’s and Schleiermacher’s competing proposals for university reform have garnered a fair deal of scholarly attention, especially by historians. However, the philosophical dimensions of these proposals, such as their connection to German idealism’s wider metaphysical, epistemological, pedagogical commitments, remain surprisingly poorly understood. Today, I wish to focus on clarifying just one of these philosophical dimensions, namely, Fichte’s and Schleiermacher’s relation to Kant. Specifically, I will argue that in their proposals for the new University of Berlin, Fichte and Schleiermacher articulate a shared internal criticism of Kant’s justification of the university in the Conflict of the Faculties, raising two interrelated problems for it.

The first is an ethical problem. In the course of defending the university, Kant must endorse the separation of what he calls the “scholarly community” from the “civil community.” Fichte and Schleiermacher, by contrast, see the university as precisely the institution tasked with overcoming the separation of these two communities, and they therefore object to Kant’s strategy of premising the university’s justification on this division.

The second problem is an institutional one. Kant is unable to demonstrate that the university is the optimal institutional means for realizing the ends he ascribes to higher education. These ends could just as well be realized by the competing French model of higher education, which replaces universities with specialized schools supplemented by academies of science. Fichte and Schleiermacher, by contrast, argue explicitly for the thesis that the university is the optimal institution for realizing the purposes of higher education. They do so by indicating that only the university can form students so that they become capable of partaking in scientific inquiry, a feature of the university that entails on their view, the unity of teaching and research and the university’s vanguard position within society.

In sum, by omitting any discussion of the university’s role in teaching and forming students, of the reciprocal effect of this formative process back onto the researcher herself, and of the university’s capacity to exemplify the ideal of unity to the rest of society by instantiating it in miniature, Kant overlooks three key arguments in defense of the university. To better defend the university and resolve the two problems facing Kant’s justification, Fichte and Schleiermacher develop a new vision of community to be realized at the University of Berlin. That is to say, their proposals focus on the special kind of community (Gemeinschaft) and reciprocity (Wechselwirkung) unique to the university, its requirements, effects, and conditions of flourishing. Their proposals thus advance an internal criticism of Kant because they share Kant’s premises that the university ought to be justified and that this justification must rest on furthering the ends of the state, but they contend that Kant’s vision of the university community is too impoverished to attain this desired justification.

The remainder of my talk will proceed in two parts. First, I will turn to Kant’s Conflict to examine in more detail his justification of the university and the separation of the scholarly and civil communities on which it depends. Second, I will turn to Fichte’s and Schleiermacher’s proposals to explicate their responses to the ethical and institutional problems arising from Kant’s justification of the university, and their alternative vision of community to be established at the new university.

2.   Kant’s Vision of Community in the Conflict of the Faculties

The Conflict of the Faculties (Der Streit der Facultäten) was published in 1798 as a collection of three self-standing “essays” (CF 243/AA 7:11). Each essay purports to address a conflict between the university’s “lower” faculty (philosophy) against one of the “higher” faculties (law, medicine, and theology). In truth, however, it is only the first essay of the Conflict, which deals with the dispute between philosophy and theology, that addresses the university as such.

This first essay was actually composed slightly earlier, in 1794, and constitutes Kant’s response to the censorship of his 1793 book Religion within in the Boundaries of Mere Reason. Unsurprisingly, Kant’s central thesis in this essay to defend the academic freedom of philosophy or the lower faculty, asserting its right to publish without oversight from the theology faculty and the state.

Kant’s general strategy will consist in deploying a utilitarian justification for the existence of the university in general and the philosophical faculty in particular, a strategy that attempts to demonstrate that by protecting the lower faculty’s academic freedom, the state will ultimately further its own interests; and, conversely, by censoring the lower faculty, the state acts against its own interests. In sum, lower faculty is justified—deserving of funding and toleration—because by pursuing truth for its own sake, it, in the long run, proves useful to the state. This is the basic structure of Kant’s justification of the university in the Conflict.

Now, I would like to examine more closely Kant’s justification of the university and bring out an important danger that it faces. 

(Qualification: Pozzo’s criticism that Kant in no way intends to provide a “general theory of the university” in Conflict. In short, (a) Kant’s contemporaries did not read him this way and (b) Kant merely means that he is considering “feasibility constraints,” which applies equally to the proposals by Fichte and Schleiermacher)

2.1.  Justifying the University

As I have mentioned, Kant’s justification of the university begins with the state.[1] Kant contends that it is the state, and not the university, that ranks among the [P4, P5] “artificial institutions [künstlichen Einrichtungen] grounded on an idea of reason [Vernunftidee]” (CF 250/AA 7:21). The university thus has, as we have seen, its ultimate justification in its contribution to the ends of the state. Consistent with this position, Kant regards the university throughout the Conflict only as a means that the state can “use for its end [Zweck]” (CF 250/AA 7:21), or insofar as the university is “beneficial [ersprießlich]” to the state (CF 261/AA 7:35) or contributes to the state’s “advantage [Vorteil]” (CF 260/AA 7:34).

According to Kant, there are two ways in which the university contributes to the ends of the state.[2] First, the higher faculties contribute directly to the ends of the state by secure the state’s influence (Einfluß) over “the people” (das Folk). According to Kant, influencing the people is a [P4] “felt need [gefühltes Bedürfniß]” for the state, and the state therefore has a right to it (CF 250/AA 7:21). Kant observes that [P6] “The government is most interested in that by which it obtains the strongest and most lasting influence over the people, and such are the objects of the higher faculties.” As Kant elaborates, the people’s “motives [Triebfedern],” by which their behavior can be influenced, are threefold, namely, their interest in their eternal well-being, their civil well-being, and their physical well-being (Wohl)—in a word, their salvation, possessions, and life. These three motives are then mapped by Kant on to the three higher faculties and, in particular, the three kinds of officials who secure these sources of well-being: clergymen, jurists, and physicians, each of whom is trained and regulated by university’s corresponding faculty. Kant concludes that higher faculties’ tripartite division therefore accords with reason (“nach Vernunft”) or corresponds to an “a priori principle” insofar as this division derives from the distinctive need of the state to influence the people and, so, ultimately from the state’s status as an idea of reason.

Yet Kant thinks that there remains one further way in which the university might contribute to the ends of the state, namely through philosophy or the lower faculty.

The lower faculty, Kant contends, contributes indirectly to the end of the state. Philosophy at the university consists in pursuing truth for its own sake, a task Kant variously characterizes as “care for the interests of the sciences” (CF 248/AA 7:18) and the “public presentation of truth” (CF 260/AA 7:33; CF 255/AA 7:28). Essential to philosophy’s truth-seeking is its practice of critique, the “test of the scholars” (CF 248/AA 7:19). This practice dictates not only that philosophy be, with respect to the state’s teachings, “free to evaluate everything” (CF 249/A 7:19–20) and free “to subject it always to the critique of reason” (CF 287/AA 7:67), but, even more radically, that “the lower faculty has […] the duty […] to see to it that everything put forward in public as a principle is true” (CF 259/AA 7:32). Philosophy at the university is therefore an essentially truth-seeking and critical enterprise, by which Kant has in mind what we today call basic research within the college of arts and sciences.

Such research, Kant maintains, provides an essential service to the state: true council with respect to its policies. By enjoying its academic freedom and unbridled pursuit of truth, the lower faculty will, in the long run, provide the state with more knowledge and “insight” than it would otherwise have. At least some of this knowledge, Kant contends, will become instrumentally valuable for the state in achieving its ends. (You can imagine, e.g., economics). This indirect usefulness to the state justifies the existence of the lower faculty. In this way, [P7, P8] the lower faculty may even become the higher: precisely because its contribution to the state’s interests is indirect, its contribution to the ends of the state might be greater. Truth proves more useful than doctrine.

Now, as we have seen, in order to provide this true council, the philosophy faculty requires not only funding from the state but, equally importantly, freedom from the state’s oversight when undertaking its public inquiry and critique. Here Kant evokes the famous slogan [P9] “laissez faire”—”leave us alone!,” demanding that, if the state wishes to advances its own interests, that it must “not hinder the progress and insights of the sciences” (CF 249/AA 7:19). If the state censors, interferes with, or in any way impedes the philosophy faculty, it acts contrary to its own interests.

Taking stock of Kant’s justification of the university, there is much in it we might commend, especially from Kant’s standpoint. Such a strong policy of academic freedom would have saved Kant’s Religion book from censorship, secured his job, and established the necessity of an independent research body funded by the state, all on grounded on the state’s own self-interest. But while Kant’s demand for academic freedom for scholarly publications might appear innocuous to us, it was in fact quite radical for Kant’s time. Indeed, Kant and his contemporaries perceived philosophy’s freedom to publish its research and criticism to be a grave danger. The reason is simple: the people are easily swayed. So, philosophy’s public and critical questioning of what are widely thought to be the basic tenets of citizens’ obedience—belief in the existence of a personal God who metes out eternal punishment and reward, the suggestion that the people might hold a right of resistance against an oppressive state, and so on—threatens to undermine their submission. Such critical writings, most intellectuals contended, could easily cause the people to disobey or act immorally, even if just out of misunderstanding. Without governmental and theological oversight of philosophy’s public writings, this danger is a real one, and it is especially acute because Kant justifies the university on solely on the grounds of its usefulness to the state, a usefulness which would clearly be undermined if it were to cause widespread disobedience. Academic freedom might not bolster utility after all.

The severity of this danger should not be overlooked. Although today we may smile at the thought that philosophical writings threaten the state’s security, in Kant’s day such a danger was considered imminent. Leading philosophers at the time held that the practice of critical thinking—the very activity that Kant sees as inextricable from philosophy and philosophizing—as capable of undermining the state. For example, Moses Mendelssohn writes that such freedom in publications “weakens the moral sentiment and leads to […] anarchy” (Moses Mendelssohn) and Friedrich Karl von Moser that such writings may “ignite and inflame” rather than “illuminate and enlighten.”[3] Kant himself acknowledges the danger of free inquiry throughout the Conflict, regularly calling the people “incompetent,” literally, idiots [Idioten], unable to think for themselves, and therefore legitimately subject to the state’s intellectual influence (CF 248/7:18) . We may call this Kant’s elitism in Conflict. As Kant puts it: [P10] “[T]here must always be statutory precepts of the government regarding teachings to be set forth in public, since unlimited freedom to proclaim any sort of opinion publicly is bound to be dangerous both to the government and to the public itself” (CF 260/AA 7:33). This pressing danger is mitigated by Kant by introducing a pivotal separation among the people into what he calls the scholarly and civil communities, and it is this division that is, on the one hand, the most notable mark of his justification of the university, and, on the other, the reason why this ultimately justification fails in the eyes of Fichte and Schleiermacher. So I turn to this division now.

2.2.  Separating the Scholarly and Civil Communities

As I have mentioned, Kant mitigates the danger of philosophy’s academic freedom by observing that the people do not form a homogeneous bloc but divide into two communities, one “scholarly” (gelehrt) and the other “civil” (bürgerlich), which, in turn, form two kinds of “public” (Publicum). Philosophy, Kant tells us, addresses its writings only to the scholarly community and not the civil one. More importantly, the civil community takes no interest in philosophy’s writings and, even if they did, they could not understand them. The state thus has no reason to worry about philosophy’s influence on the people insofar as they form the civil community, since it can expect no uptake by them. The state can thus safely grant philosophy its freedom without risking disobedience.

Here is how Kant summarizes this separation:

[P11] The higher faculties […] must answer to the government only for the instruction and information they give their businessmen to expound to the public; for these circulate among the public [Publicum] as a civil community [bürgerliches gemeines Wesen] and, because they could impair the government’s influence over it, are subject to its sanction. On the other hand, the teachings and views that the faculties, as theorists, have to settle with one another are directed to a different kind of public – a scholarly community [gelehrten gemeinen Wesens] devoted to the sciences; and since the people are resigned to understanding nothing about this, the government does not see fit to intervene in scholarly discussions. (CF 260–61/AA 7:34)

This is precisely the moment in the justification of the university where the later idealists part ways with Kant. For here it becomes evident that, if Kant’s defense of the university is to succeed, it must suppose a pernicious elitism: not only the indifference but also the incapacity of the civil community to understand and care about science and philosophy. This dependence can be seen by considering the counter-factual: if the scholarly and civil communities were not separate, then philosophy’s academic freedom would in fact influence the people, and, indeed, likely in a negative way from the state’s point of view. Since Kant’s justification of the university rests on its contribution to the utility of the state, such a unification of the two communities would undermine his defense of the university. Yet, for Fichte and Schleiermacher, realizing this unification is an essential part of the university’s vocation; making the university’s justification depend on preserving this rift is perverse, if not self-defeating.

However, before moving to Fichte’s and Schleiermacher’s internal criticism of Kant, it is worthwhile to examine one further feature of Kant’s separation of the two communities in Conflict, namely that it marks a departure from his related and better-known distinction between public and private reason.

Kant’s theory of public reason, developed most notably in What is Enlightenment? and What does It Mean to Orient Oneself in Thinking?, is a social role theory. The public use of reason denotes a social role that anyone can adopt, regardless of her position in society. So long as she directs her arguments to the right mental faculty (reason, not inclination) and adopts the proper medium (published writings, not shouting in the town square), she exercises her reason publicly or acts “as a scholar” (WE 18/AA 8:37). Kant’s theory of public reason is a dynamic theory, permitting one to use reason publicly at one time, privately at another.

 By contrast, Kant’s distinction between the two communities in the Conflict is a theory of social stations. Not just anyone can be a member of the scholarly community; this privilege is reserved for graduates of the university, closed off to what Kant calls the “great unthinking masses” (WE 18/AA 8:36). Put differently, to be a member of the scholarly community is just to be a university graduate; otherwise, one belongs to the civil community. Consequently, membership is mutually exclusive and static. One cannot be a university graduate by day and a non-graduate by night.

With that said, I now turn to the two problems for Kant’s justification of the university raised by Fichte and Schleiermacher.

(While Kant’s departure from a social role defense of freedom in research and publication to a social station justification of the same may appear like a large-scale shift, seeds of Kant’s later position lie latent in his earlier one. Throughout Enlightenment and Orientation, Kant maintains the same dim view of the people, establishing the need for the state to censor popular writings. More importantly, in these texts Kant maintains a sharp contrast between “civil” (bürgerlich) freedom and the “spiritual” (geistig) freedom of thought (WE 22/AA 8:41–42; Kant 1996c, 16/AA 8:144), which he identifies as two mutually exclusive kinds of freedom. This earlier contrast between civil and spiritual freedom prefigures Kant’s later distinction between the scholarly and civil communities, which similarly exclude one another. Kant’s new vocabulary in Conflict of “communal being” (gemeine Wesen), if we presume its connection to the concept of community (Gemeinschaft) developed in his critical system, provides further evidence that he intended membership in the two communities to be mutually exclusive and to therefore depart from his older view.)

(One may doubt Kant’s sincerity. Pasquarè (2024), for example, challenges the shift in Kant’s views as I have described it, which he deems the “prevalent reading” (433), arguing instead that Kant’s elitism is merely “parodying” the absolutist vocabulary espoused by the state (440). In Conflict, Pasquarè concludes, Kant turns “absolutist concepts and practices against absolutism itself” by showing how even if we adopt the most dismal view of the people, we can still safeguard the university’s utility to the state and its role in enlightenment (434).[4])

3.   Two Problems for Kant’s Vision of Community: Fichte and Schleiermacher

We have already begun to see, I think, some of the cracks in Kant’s justification of the university in the eyes of Fichte and Schleiermacher. I will now present their criticisms in more detail. Specifically, the two idealists, I argue, raise two problems for Kant’s argument.

First is the ethical problem. Kant’s justification of the university rests on a pernicious separation of the scholarly and civil communities, and he fails to identify in concreto how the university is to contribute to overcoming this division. The university thus leaves the separation intact, and this is an ethical failure.

Second is the institutional problem. Even if Kant’s utility argument successfully legitimates scientific research, it fails to establish that the university, as opposed to other “higher scientific establishments” such as the academies and polytechnical schools, is the optimal institution for performing this task.

To remedy these two deficiencies, Fichte and Schleiermacher articulate a more robust vision of community to be established at the new University of Berlin. This new vision of community centers on the university’s effects on its students, on the one hand, and the effects on its researchers and the wider community, on the other. I will begin with the ethical problem.

(Before proceeding to a more detailed discussion of Fichte and Schleiermacher, it is worth considering the degree to which they knew Kant’s Conflict, as this is an issue that is occasionally contested in the literature.)[5]

3.1.  The Ethical Problem

Both Fichte and Schleiermacher accept Kant’s theory of human progress and its promise of an eventual convergence of the scholarly and civil communities. But they depart from him by identifying the university as having a concrete role to play in facilitating this progress and that this role in social progress also contributes to its justification. An essential part of this role consists in forming the student herself, her education to Wissenschaft and its subsequent knock-on effects—dimensions of the university unexamined by Kant and that can be summarized by the dual values of Bildung and accessibility. To advance these aims, the idealists advocate for a more radical vision of the community of knowledge and students to be established at the new University of Berlin than Kant’s “scholarly” one, in which students and teachers form a [P12, P13] “learning organic whole [lernenden organischen Ganzen]” (Fichte, DP §11, 81/GA 2,11:97) or a “community of knowledge [Gemeinschaft des Wissens]” (Schleiermacher, OT 5/KGA 1,6:23; cf. LPE §146, 81/E 107). To better understand the sort of community of knowledge and students they envision, we can examine each of these values in turn.

For both idealists, the university is distinguished from the lower schools because it inculcates in students not knowledge of various subjects or what we today might call “settled knowledge,” but the capacity to think in general—what we today call critical thinking. For Fichte, this capacity is termed the “art of using the understanding scientifically” [Kunst des wissenschaftlichen Verstandesgebrauches] (DP §5, 175/GA 2,11:87; cf. ET 261/GA 2,9:360), by which he means the student’s capacity to creatively learn new areas of knowledge, grasp its position within the totality of science, and apply it to novel cases. In university education, “the final aim,” Fichte insists, “is not knowledge, but rather the art of using knowledge” (DP §5, 174/GA 2,11:86); it aims “not at learning, but at the formation [Bildung] of the ability to learn” (DP §5, 175/GA 2,11:87).

Similarly, for Schleiermacher, university education consists in [P14] “learning how to learn” and [P15] “forming the individual into knowledge.” As Schleiermacher elaborates, learning to learn means focusing on students’ skills and disposition rather than information. Given the skills-based nature of university education, students naturally require significantly less time to complete their university studies compared to their long duration in the lower schools. For Fichte and Schleiermacher, university instruction is not so much a matter of learning facts, but of cultivating a certain disposition and capacity vis-à-vis knowledge as such.

That being said, Fichte and Schleiermacher do identify a particular subject-matter that is essential to university instruction: the philosophical encyclopedia. While specialization in the “real” (Schleiermacher) or “empirical” (Fichte) sciences is open to students later in their studies (OT 17/KGA 1,6:36; DP §44, 224/GA 2,11:135–36), they must begin their university education with an overview of the systematic totality of knowledge. This overview is best gained by surveying the basic outlines of each discipline in encyclopedic form, which, because it identifies the interconnectedness of science, expresses the genuine “philosophical” (Fichte) or “scientific” (Schleiermacher) “spirit” (DP §19, 194/GA 2,11:105–6; OT 17/KGA 1,6:36; cf. OT 24/KGA 1,6:43). If science constitutes a systematic whole, such that each discipline (Fach) can only be understood in its relation to the others, then its propaedeutic can only be encyclopedic. Although complete, this encyclopedia is not exhaustive; it must leave the details of each domain to the particular sciences themselves, which are by their nature inexhaustible. For Fichte, students should hear lectures on the Wissenschaftslehre, as the “science of science,” as this general science reflects not only on the possibility of thinking as such, but also provides the first principles for each particular science (GA 1,2:117). For Schleiermacher, it is “dialectic” to which students should be exposed, which, as the “organon of all the sciences” (SD 7/KGA 2,10:8) also includes an “architectonic” of their first principles (SD 1/KGA 2,10:5). Such dialectic, Schleiermacher contends, provides students an “immediate intuition of reason and its activity,” in which the “supposed opposition between reason and experience, between speculation and empiricism is destroyed,” thereby opening up students to specialized study of nature and history through philosophy (OT 27/KGA 1,6:46–47). In sum, encountering the philosophical encyclopedia constitutes students’ introduction to Wissenschaft, in which not only the knowers but knowledge itself forms a community.

The community of scientific material to be assimilated by students is also reflected in the communal form of its reception. For both Fichte and Schleiermacher, it is essential that students at the university constitute a unified student body (DP §8, 177/GA 2,11:89; OT 28/KGA 1,6:48). They do so by engaging in reciprocal communication (Mitteling) and collaboration (Mitwirkung) with one another and with the teacher (OT §1, 1/KGA 1,6:19; DP §8/GA 2,11:89; ET 261/GA 2,9:360). Formation of this scientific community occurs not only in the lecture hall or in the seminar room but throughout the university, including the students’ residences. It is therefore imperative that students temporarily be separated from their ordinary lives, especially their parents’ homes. For this reason, both Fichte and Schleiermacher argue that it is preferable for students to travel to Berlin from other cities; special care must be taken to ensure that Berliners are sufficiently independent of the influence of their family (DP §10, 182/GA 2,11:94; ET 262/GA 1,10:364; OT 76/KGA 1,6:96–97).

Finally, for Fichte and Schleiermacher it is essential for everyone to be given the opportunity to become students at the university—that there be the “most widespread scientific formation [wissenschaftliche Bildung] as possible (OT 7/KGA 1,6:25). To be sure, because of the great difficulties of scholarship, the scientific spirit cannot be said to “arise in everyone” (OT 24/KGA 1,6:43) or be “expected of all” (DP 186, §12/GA 2,11:97–98). This paucity of scientific talent, along with the scarcity of educational resources in their time, lends their argument for universal access to the university an epistemic character. Success in science requires not only great talent and mental ability but also a certain capacity for creativity and critique. These skills are not only rare but also difficult to discern in youths, especially since their opportunities for demonstrating these skills in the lower schools are limited by the mechanical instruction used therein. Everyone of some merit, then, should be welcome to study at the university, to test their scientific mettle, as it were, in the forge of academic freedom; the minority of students who find themselves called to scientific life can continue on at the university, while the remaining graduates can seek jobs in civil society or posts as civil servants. Because university education concerns the formation of the student as such, it is beneficial even for those students who do not wish to become scholars themselves (OT 26/KGA 1,6:44–45). In order to make these benefits truly accessible to everyone, Fichte and Schleiermacher even propose robust systems of financial aid (DP §§51–52, 237–40/GA 2,11:145–49; OT 42/KGA 1,6:62), which would, as Fichte puts it, ensure that “every talent, even without any equivalent in money, finds development with us” (DP §51, 237/GA 2,11:147). Fichte and Schleiermacher thus do not merely hope for everyone to take an interest in science but hold the university responsible for initiating the public, as many and as far as possible, to this standpoint.

(This community of students exhibits three characteristics salient to remedying the ethical problem facing Kant’s justification of the university. [1] beyond lectures—new instructional methods—“discussion periods” (Conversatorien), [2] language—Fichte and Schleiermacher place language (Sprache) at the center of their accounts of the university’s student community; [3], accessibility—for Fichte and Schleiermacher it is essential for everyone to be given the opportunity to be students at the university)

Although our discussion of Fichte’s and Schleiermacher’s account of the community of students and knowledge at the university has been brief, we can nonetheless glean from it how they intended to overcome the ethical problem in Kant’s justification of the university. By participating in the “community of knowledge” (Schleiermacher) or the “spiritual communal life” (Fichte) at university, students acquire a certain kind of scientific formation, one which is conducive to collaboration, mutual communication, and mutual respect. This formation, they contend, can only arise by participating in the communal project of seeking knowledge for its own sake. It should in principle be open to everyone, and, in the course of human progress, all future members of the civil community should come to attend the university, even if they have little interest in becoming scholars themselves [P16] (DP §11, 181/GA 2,11:97). In this way, Kant’s posited gulf between the civil and scholarly communities is gradually bridged, as members of each community enjoy a “shared common life” at the university, ameliorating possible conflict between the two communities not only because graduates retain a memory of the experienced unity throughout their lives but also because they are now equipped to be life-long participants in scientific research and communication. This popularization of higher education overturns Kant’s elitism.

(Moreover, the university’s gradual overcoming of the separation of the two communities occurs precisely in the manner condemned by Kant in Conflict, namely, as “bottom-up” progress (CF 307–8/AA 7:92–93))

3.2.  The Institutional Problem

Having established the importance of the values of Bildung and accessibility in overcoming the separation of the scholarly and civil communities posited by Kant, there remain two further questions raised by Fichte and Schleiermacher that are overlooked in Kant’s justification of the university: first, why is the university the best institutional means for realizing these values? Second, what are the university’s institutional effects on researchers and wider society? The idealists’ answers to these two questions are interrelated: the university’s desirability as an institutional form is due partially to its effects on research and society that only it can bring about. That being said, they also identify features intrinsic to the university itself that make it a necessary institution of modern society.

Regarding the first question, the idealists aim to justify the university’s claim to being the optimal institution for attaining the purposes of higher education and scientific research—actualizing student Bildung, on the one hand, and benefiting research and society in its distinctive way, on the other—by focusing on its indispensable role as an intermediary educational institution. Like Humboldt, Fichte and Schleiermacher see the division of education into its three institutions—the school, the university, and the academy—as pertaining to “the nature of the matter [Natur der Sache]” itself (Humboldt 2017, 118/GS 10:141; cf. Humboldt 2017, 113/GS 10:256; DP §13, 187/GA 2,11:98–99; DP §56, 241/GA 2,11:152; OT 15/KGA 1,6:34; cf. LPE §165–70, 84–85/E 111–13). Their contention is that Bildung and its concomitant effects can only be realized in such an intermediary institution. Critique and basic research, for instance, they contend, are best fostered at the university. This is because the purpose of schools is to present facts and settled knowledge without critique, whereas the purpose of academies lies in propelling and digesting new discoveries on the basis of shared philosophical foundations. This basic idea, that the university is necessary because of its mediating institutional role, underlies Fichte and Schleiermacher’s justification of it.

(Defensive posture. Fichte: “What the [the university] has hitherto been no longer has the right to exist” (DP §5, 175/GA 2,11:87). But this “intermediary” argument in support of the university it not yet sufficient for justifying it. Even if we grant the necessity of a “middle” institution within higher education, it remains unsettled that the university, as opposed to an alternative model of such a mediating institution, should serve this function. The most prominent alternative at the time was the French model of specialized higher schools developed by Napoleon. This alternative model replaced universities with specialized professional and polytechnical schools (écoles) that also stood in-between the lower secondary schools (lycées) and the higher academies of science (academies). The promise of such specialized schools put substantial pressure on the Kantian-utilitarian justification of the university. If the university's legitimacy rests solely on its contribution to the state’s utility, and if the lower faculty's value lies only in truth-seeking—ignoring effects on students, researchers, and society writ large—then specialized schools and academies may prove superior, since specialization should, after all, yield gains in efficiency.

Responding to this new problem, Fichte and Schleiermacher underscore the university’s unique capacity to foster community in four respects—with respect to knowledge, students, researchers, and society writ large. In the previous subsection, we examined the ethical import of the first two communities (knowledge and students); now I would like to examine the effects of the last two (researchers and society).

The first communal knock-on effect that Fichte and Schleiermacher deploy in their justification of the university concerns the famed unity of teaching and research, an essential pillar of the Humboldtian university (Humboldt 2017, 113/GS 10:256–57). Although Fichte and Schleiermacher do not deploy language as concise as Humboldt to describe this unity, they do endorse it. Put simply, this doctrine holds that the researcher’s teaching duties, rather than detracting from her scientific task, enliven and impel her research. Fichte and Schleiermacher maintain this doctrine even while recognizing that university teaching is a time-consuming occupation—Schleiermacher even characterizes university teaching as “living with youths” (Scheliermacher, OT 21/KGA 1,6:40), (DP §54, 239/GA 2,11:150; DP §40, 219/GA 2,11:130; cf. OT 47/KGA 1,6:65–66).

University teaching spurs research, they contend, for two reasons. First, it pertains to the “art of lecturing” that, by recreating one’s course through knowledge in front of a critical audience, the researcher reformulates her thoughts and improves them. University lectures provide numerous opportunities for this productive repetition.

(For any “true master of science,” Schleiermacher contends, “no repetition is possible without some new combination enlivening oneself, a new discovery drawing one to it; one will always be learning by teaching, and will always stand as living and truly producing before one’s listeners” (OT 30/KGA 1,6:49). Fichte similarly remarks that, in lectures, university teachers have long “communicated orally their struggle for something more perfect, in order […] by this interaction [Wechselwirkung] with others to become clear in themselves concerning the proposed book” (DP §3, 173/GA 2,11:85))

Second, because of the special character of this audience—youths introducing themselves to science, some of whom are themselves budding researchers—and the regularity of the teacher’s interaction with these students, especially in seminars (OT 40–41/KGA 1,6:60–61), university teaching provides an especially ripe environment for fostering fresh research, outstripping the society of old masters found in scientific academies, at least with respect prospect of novelty. Thus, the special kind of community to be established at the university provides a further benefit overlooked by Kant: it facilitates research.

The second knock-on effect of the university community is its capacity to be a vanguard or model institution for the rest of society. Both idealists deploy religious metaphors to describe this higher purpose of the university. The speak of the university as if it were a secularized monastery or a scholarly city on a hill. The university’s is a “higher” and “sacred” purpose, they claim (Fichte), a “sanctuary” of science (Schleiermacher). They justify their use of religious metaphors by portraying the university as living proof that spiritual, communal life in the name of higher values is not only possible for human beings but actual—the university is the visible scientific union. Extollation of such lofty aims of scholarship will be familiar to readers of German idealism. But what is striking in both idealists’ defense of the university, I think, is their focus on the ethical effects of the community to be established at the new university, not merely of the virtues of the individual scholar.

We have already seen how Fichte and Schleiermacher understand science itself to be an intrinsically communal undertaking, essentially marked by collaboration and communication. Since knowledge (Wissen) forms a systematic whole, such that each of its branches garners its validity and meaning only in relation to the other branches and to the whole itself, it follows that no single researcher can exhaust this totality alone. Thus, the systematic investigation of knowledge (Wissenschaft) must be cooperative. “In scarcely any other area of human action,” Schleiermacher remarks, “is there such an extended community, such a steady progression of tradition from the very outset, as is to be found in science” (OT 2–3/KGA 1,6:21). Moreover, because science is essentially communal, we may also infer, according to Fichte and Schleiermacher, that it is public, “present in cultured peoples as a whole as their communal work [gemeinschaftliches Werk] and possession” (OT 12/KGA 1,6:31). As Fichte puts it, science belongs to “the living spirit of the age,” not to any particular person (DP §62, 248/GA 2,11:159). The communal nature of research and teaching, best realized by the intermediary institution of the university, thus provides the people with a vanguard model of a unified community to be realized politically, deriving from their own common and public intellectual activity.

As a closing note, it is fascinating to see how this political dimension of the university’s scientific vanguard community was not lost on Fichte and Schleiermacher. Schleiermacher announces in the context of German unification that [P17] “the community in which [German] states must stand should nowhere be expressed more intimately than in scientific matters,” turning the university’s community of science to a “preparatory means” for national political unity (OT 7/KGA 1,6:25). This community, he contends, exhibits precisely the kind of “inner unity” that the German states should realize (ibid.), making it not only a [P18] “transition point” for students (OT 16/KGA 1,6:35), but for society as well (OT 80/KGA 1,6:100). Fichte concludes his proposal by observing that the university provides an “image” (Bild) of both domestic and international political unity, exhibiting the unity of divided political power at home and peaceful competition abroad (DP §67/GA 2,11:169). Striking in both proposals is the future orientation of the university’s community: its kind of unity proceeds in advance of the society, serving as “preparation” (Schleiermacher) or “prophecy” (Fichte) for this wider-spread political unification (DP §67, 258–59/GA 2,11:169–70; OT 56/KGA 1,6:75–76). Laying aside their nationalist dimension, both proposals see the progress of humanity, including bridging the gap between the scholarly and the popular, as a matter to be actualized bottom-up in large part by the university itself.

To conclude, I have tried to demonstrate that Fichte’s and Schleiermacher’s proposals for the new University of Berlin are remarkably aligned along three dimensions: first, they raise and attempt to resolve two problems for Kant’s justification of the university, which I have named the ethical and institutional problems; second, that in posing these two problems, they undertake an internal criticism of Kant’s justification of the university by demonstrating that his own assumptions about the value and purpose of the university should propel him beyond the normative limitations posed by his elitism; and, third, that both of these internal criticisms turn on the distinctive kind of community to be established by the new University of Berlin.

By focusing on the similarities of Fichte’s and Schleiermacher’s proposals, we have seen that both idealists are engaged in the shared practice of institutional design. Their task was to determine the optimal features of the university as a meso-level social structure and to establish the feasibility of its continued existence, while also defending it against competing visions of the same structure. In doing so, they examine the university’s capacity to affect transformations in students, science, and society, primarily by reenvisioning the Kantian categories of community and reciprocity. Beyond merely asserting the intrinsic value of higher education, the two idealists deploy these categories to conceptualize the collective nature of knowledge, science, and research, and the unifying political value of the university. So long as the design of the institutions of higher education remains a pressing one for philosophy today, it is worthwhile to revisit their attempts at this difficult and peculiar task, and the possible pitfalls of resting the university’s justification solely on basic research.
